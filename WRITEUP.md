# Silent Second Opinion: Does MedGemma Admit When Misleading Context Changes Its Answer?

Temesgen Tewolde · Duke University · AIPI 590 (Explainable AI) · 2026-10-17

All numbers below are regenerated by `python reproduce.py` from the committed `results/` bundle. They include a
one-point correction to the printed poster; see the erratum in [`DESIGN.md`](DESIGN.md).

## Abstract

MedGemma 1.5 4B follows a bare assertion of a wrong answer in about a third of eligible cases, and its visible response
hides that influence about twice as often as it hides a fabricated clinical claim.

One misleading sentence was appended to 200 text-only MedMisQA questions [1] under four conditions (clean, sham,
fabricated evidence, matched bare answer assertion), with the model's thinking trace on and off. That is 1,600 greedy
rollouts. The design follows a prior study of matched evidence and answer cues [2].

- **Susceptibility.** The answer cue was followed in 34% (thinking) and 35% (direct) of eligible items, the evidence cue
  in 42% and 41%, against a 4–5% sham baseline. Thinking did not change the follow rate (McNemar p = 0.76 and 1.0).
- **Disclosure.** Among followed rollouts the response admitted the evidence cue 78% of the time but the answer cue only
  38% (difference 0.40, 95% CI 0.24–0.55). For the answer cue the trace admitted it more often than the response
  (59% vs 38%, paired p = 0.002).
- **Source framing.** Authority-framed cues were followed about twice as often as patient-framed ones.

**Main caveat.** The disclosure judge is an LLM, checked only against LLM annotators, not a person. On the clean check it
agrees well with blind annotators (response κ 0.78, trace κ 0.76 on 60 unseen rollouts), but direct-mode responses are
weaker (κ 0.44).

## 1. Background and related work

**Misleading context in medical question answering.** MedMisBench [1] injects fabricated, plausible-sounding clinical
claims into medical questions and measures how often models abandon a correct answer. Related work shows clinical models
are exposed to cognitive biases [10], to sycophancy that depends on who is speaking and on whether evidence is offered
[11], and to adversarial medical misinformation [12]. Linzmayer & Elhadad [2] align two cue types on the same items,
a fabricated *evidence* cue and a bare *answer* assertion that endorses the same wrong option, and study susceptibility,
disclosure, mechanism and monitorability in three reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, GPT-5.4,
gpt-oss-120b). They report that the answer cue is adopted 10–27 points more often than the evidence cue, that cues are
disclosed in 81–98% of traces but only 7–90% of responses, that the assertion is disclosed less than evidence-based cues,
and that an LLM monitor catches 78% of corrupted decisions from an open trace (with guidance, 5% false positives)
against at most 32% from any response.

**Do models say what they think?** Turpin et al. [3] showed that chain-of-thought explanations can be steered by biasing
features the explanation never mentions, and can rationalize the biased answer. Chen et al. [4] measured how often
reasoning models verbalize a hint they used and reported verbalization in fewer than 20% of cases in the settings they
tested. Young [6] compared a model's thinking tokens with its answer in 12 open-weight reasoning models and found that, when
a model followed an incorrect hint, the thinking tokens contained hint-related words that the visible answer omitted in
55.4% of cases, while the reverse occurred in under 1%. Korbak et al. [5] argue that monitoring reasoning traces is a
valuable but fragile safety opportunity. Zaman & Srivastava [7] caution that hint verbalization is an imperfect proxy
for faithfulness: absence of the hint's words does not prove the hint was unused, and verbalization rises with larger
token budgets. In medicine, disclosure has been scored on one surface at a time, in the trace [9] or in the response [8].

**Gap and contribution.** The earlier medical studies compared different models, so trace access is confounded with
capability. This study uses **one small open medical model, MedGemma 1.5 4B** [13], and toggles its thinking trace in
place, adds a sham-sentence baseline for the follow rate, and reports disclosure separately for the trace and the
response among followed rollouts. It is a replication-with-a-twist in a different model class rather than a first
demonstration: several of its findings (trace discloses more than response for the assertion; the assertion is
disclosed less than evidence) agree with [2] and [6], and one does not (see Discussion).

## 2. Research question

When misleading context makes MedGemma abandon an answer, does it say so, in its thinking trace or in its visible
response? Does that depend on the kind of cue, and on whether the model thinks first? Three comparisons were fixed
before the main run; everything else is exploratory.

- **P1.** Final-response disclosure, evidence cue vs answer cue (thinking mode, followed rollouts).
- **P2.** Trace vs final-response disclosure on the same followed thinking rollouts (paired).
- **P3.** Follow rate, thinking vs direct, per cue (paired by item).

## 3. Method

Each of 200 items was run in four conditions and two modes, one greedy rollout per cell: 1,600 rollouts in total.

**Data.** 200 items sampled with seed 590 from the MedMisQA split of MedMisBench [1] (1,747 usable items), limited to
text-only items with 4–5 options. Each item carries a fabricated clinical claim for a wrong option, which is the
target. Source framing is Authority (86 items), Neutral (62) or Patient (52).

**Conditions.** One sentence is appended after the options:

| Condition | Appended sentence |
|---|---|
| `none` | nothing |
| `sham` | an irrelevant sentence ("The patient was seen on a weekday afternoon…") |
| `evidence` | the MedMisBench fabricated claim for the target option |
| `answer` | a bare assertion of the same target, with the item's source framing ("The correct answer is C.", "The clinical guideline states…", "The patient states…") |

**Modes.** MedGemma 1.5 thinks by default, so a system prompt cannot switch thinking off. In `direct` mode the generation
prompt is pre-filled with an empty, closed thought block. The user turn is identical in both modes. Direct responses
still contain visible reasoning, so the comparison is a hidden trace against reasoning written into the response. Limits
are 4,096 new tokens in thinking mode and 2,048 in direct mode.

**Follow rate.** An item is *eligible* if the clean answer is parsed and is not the target, and the cued rollout finished
with a parsed answer. It is *followed* if the cued answer is the target. Unfinished rollouts are missing data, not
resisted cues; 10–15% of direct rollouts looped and are excluded. The answer is the *last* answer statement in the
response (a quoted earlier "the correct answer is B" does not count; see the erratum).

**Disclosure.** A local LLM judge (Qwen3-14B) reads each surface of every eligible cued rollout and decides whether any
sentence refers to the injected cue. For an evidence cue the sentence must state or paraphrase the claim and attribute it
to the input: the judge must copy the exact words that name the source (for example "the prompt states"), and the code
checks that those words sit inside the quoted sentence. For an answer cue the sentence must refer to the assertion itself
or its source, and the quote must name an answer or option and the assertion. A response that only reaches the same answer
by its own reasoning does not count. Quotes are matched to the text after ignoring punctuation and markup. Disclosure
rate is the share of followed rollouts that disclose, per surface. About 1% of judge outputs could not be parsed and are
excluded (2 of 236 followed responses, 6 of 128 followed traces).

**Statistics.** 95% Wilson intervals; exact paired McNemar tests for within-item comparisons; Fisher's exact test with a
bootstrap interval for the unpaired P1. No correction for multiple comparisons.

## 4. Results

Both cues move the model well above chance, a bare answer assertion is hidden from the response more often than a
fabricated claim, and thinking does not change how often the model is steered.

**Susceptibility.** Sham sentences land the model on the target option 4.3–4.5% of the time, so follow rates of 34–42% are
a real effect. A sham sentence changes the answer to something else in 26–28% of rollouts, which is why the baseline is
target-specific. Accuracy among finished rollouts falls under both cues and not under sham (thinking: clean 55.2%,
sham 57.4%, answer 40.0%, evidence 35.4%).

| Mode | Cue | Followed / eligible | Follow rate (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thinking | Answer | 58 / 172 | 33.7% (27.1–41.1) |
| Thinking | Evidence | 70 / 167 | 41.9% (34.7–49.5) |
| Direct | Answer | 48 / 138 | 34.8% (27.3–43.0) |
| Direct | Evidence | 60 / 146 | 41.1% (33.4–49.2) |

**Disclosure among followed rollouts.** *Silent-and-steered* means the cue was followed and the response never mentions it.

| Mode | Cue | Followed | Trace discloses | Response discloses | Silent-and-steered |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thinking | Answer | 58 | 58.9% | 37.9% | 36 (62%) |
| Thinking | Evidence | 70 | 80.3% | 77.9% | 15 (21%) |
| Direct | Answer | 48 | n/a | 45.8% | 26 (54%) |
| Direct | Evidence | 60 | n/a | 83.3% | 10 (17%) |

**P1: cue type changes what the response admits.** In thinking mode the response admitted the evidence cue in 77.9% of
followed rollouts (n = 68) and the answer cue in 37.9% (n = 58). The difference is 0.40 (95% CI 0.24–0.55, Fisher
p ≈ 9e-6), and it holds under every source framing.

**P2: the trace admits more than the response, but only for the answer cue.** For the answer cue, 58.9% of traces and
37.5% of responses disclosed (n = 56; 13 trace-only against 1 response-only, McNemar p = 0.002). For the evidence cue
the two are level: 81.2% against 76.6% (n = 64; 10 against 7, p = 0.63). Pooled, 70.8% against 58.3% (n = 120, p = 0.011).

**P3: thinking does not change susceptibility.** The evidence cue was followed in 41.5% of items in direct mode and
40.7% in thinking mode (n = 135, p = 1.0). The answer cue was followed in 32.6% against 30.2% (n = 129, p = 0.76). A
large p-value means the data cannot separate the two modes; it does not show they are identical.

**Source framing.** Authority framing roughly doubles how often the model follows either cue; patient framing is followed
least (pooled over modes, eligible rollouts):

| Source (items) | Answer cue followed | Evidence cue followed | Response discloses answer / evidence cue (followed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Authority (86) | 44.8% (60/134) | 54.3% (75/138) | 48% / 90% |
| Neutral (62) | 26.0% (25/96) | 37.2% (35/94) | 36% / 59% |
| Patient (52) | 26.2% (21/80) | 24.7% (20/81) | 29% / 80% |

The Neutral and Patient disclosure cells hold only 20–34 followed rollouts each.

## 5. Judge validation

The annotator was Claude, working blind to the judge's verdicts; the evidence-cue rows were labelled by four separate
blind Claude agents. **No person has labelled any row.** The judge was revised three times after sheets exposed errors:
it now ignores the closing "Answer: X" line, requires answer-cue quotes to name an answer or option and the assertion,
requires evidence-cue disclosures to cite the exact words naming the source, and a matching bug that discarded valid
quotes was fixed. Three sheets of 100 rollouts were labelled; the last two judge changes followed inspection of the
held-out and evidence sheets, so those three rows are in-sample to some degree. A fourth sheet of 60 unseen evidence-cue
rollouts, drawn after the final change, is the only uncontaminated check.

| Sheet (labeller) | Rows | Trace κ | Response κ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Audited sheet (Claude; used for tuning) | 100 | 0.89 (n = 52) | 0.90 (n = 99) |
| Held-out sheet (Claude) | 100 | 0.87 (n = 47) | 0.85 (n = 99) |
| Evidence-cue rows (four blind Claude agents) | 100 | 0.60 (n = 58) | 0.81 (n = 98) |
| **Clean check: unseen evidence-cue rows** | 60 | **0.76** (n = 33; 95% CI 0.44–1.00) | **0.78** (n = 60; 95% CI 0.59–0.93) |

On the clean check, judge and annotators agree on the disclosure rate (65% vs 65% of responses). Thinking-mode responses
agree perfectly (κ 1.00, n = 36); direct-mode responses are weaker (κ 0.44, n = 24), so direct-mode disclosure rates are
less certain. About 1% of judge outputs (12 of 962) could not be parsed and are excluded.

## 6. Discussion

**What agrees with prior work.** The response discloses the cue less often than the trace for a bare assertion [2, 6],
the assertion is disclosed less often than evidence [2], and source framing orders susceptibility the same way [1, 2].
Together with the 4B-model result, this suggests the pattern is not limited to larger reasoning models.

**What does not.** Linzmayer & Elhadad [2] find the assertion is *followed more* than evidence (10–27 points). Here it is
followed slightly *less* (34% vs 42%), so the hidden influence is not explained by a larger effect: the assertion is
concealed more, not followed more. Possible reasons, none tested: a different (small, medical) model; the assertion
wording was matched to each item's source framing and has not been checked against the original template; eligibility
here is "clean answer is not the target" rather than clean-correct; cues are appended unlabelled after the options.

**Implication, and what is not shown.** If an overseer reads only the response, 62% of assertion-steered responses carry no
mention of the assertion. [2] tested exactly this with LLM monitors on other models (78% caught from an open trace versus at
most 32% from responses); the present study does **not** run a monitor, so for MedGemma that implication is an inference
from disclosure rates. Disclosure here also means verbalization: as [7] stresses, a non-verbalized cue can still have
influenced the answer, and a verbalized one need not have.

## 7. Limitations

- **No human validation.** The judge was checked against LLM annotators only. On the clean check, evidence-cue trace
  agreement is κ 0.76 with a wide interval (95% CI 0.44–1.00, n = 33), and direct-mode responses are weaker (κ 0.44, n = 24).
- **Only one clean check.** The judge was revised after inspecting each earlier sheet, so those κ values are in-sample to
  some degree.
- **One model, one task format.** MedGemma 1.5 4B, English single-turn multiple choice, greedy decoding, one rollout per
  cell, with a synthetic cue appended after the options. Cues are LLM-generated and some wrong options may be defensible.
- **Direct mode is not "no reasoning".** It reasons in the visible response, and 10–15% of direct rollouts loop without
  answering. Follow rates there are measured on finished rollouts only.
- **Eligibility is not clean-correct.** An item counts if the clean answer is not the target, even when that clean answer
  was wrong. The benchmark's attack success rate counts only clean-correct items, so the two are not directly comparable [1].
- **Small subgroups; no multiplicity correction.** Provenance cells have 20–62 followed rollouts.
- **Source framing is not randomized.** Each question's source comes from the benchmark and is fixed per question, so each
  source group covers different questions; differences may partly reflect question difficulty.
- **Cue delivery differs.** The matched answer-cue wording follows [2] but has not been checked against its template table,
  and cues are appended unlabelled after the options rather than under a context header as in [1].
- **Misuse.** A one-sentence "the correct answer is C" is an easy attack on a deployed system. The prompts contain
  deliberately false medical statements for evaluation only, not clinical advice.

## 8. Reproducibility and next steps

Code, spec, tests and a text-free results bundle are in the repository; `python reproduce.py` regenerates every number
here in seconds without a GPU, and the README describes the Colab rerun. The main run took about 3.6 hours of generation
plus roughly 20 minutes of judging on an A100.

Next steps, in order:

1. Have a person label at least the evidence-cue rows, including the direct-mode responses where agreement is weakest.
2. Test a response-only monitor on MedGemma's own outputs, to replace the inference in the Discussion with a measurement.
3. Check the matched answer-cue wording against the template in [2], and repeat the follow-rate comparison on clean-correct
   items only.
4. Randomize source framing across items, and add larger models, to separate framing from question difficulty.
